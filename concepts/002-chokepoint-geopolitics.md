# 002 · Chokepoint geopolitics

*Filed 16 September 2026 — Bab al-Mandeb; Hormuz at four transits*

## Definition
    

How control over a narrow passage that disproportionate traffic must use converts into political power — and why that power is far easier to use for denial than for gain. The holder of a strait can impose costs on the world. It cannot generally make the world do anything.

Lineage
    

**Alfred Thayer Mahan** , _The Influence of Sea Power Upon History_ (1890), is the founding text: naval supremacy rests on holding the narrow seas through which commerce must pass, not on territory. The British imperial system was the applied version — Gibraltar, Suez, Aden, Malacca, Hormuz, the Cape — a chain of locks rather than a map of colonies.

**Halford Mackinder** (1904) supplied the land-power counterargument: railways would make the Eurasian interior immune to sea control. **Nicholas Spykman** split the difference with the rimland.

The modern empirical frame is the US Energy Information Administration's _World Oil Transit Chokepoints_ series, the origin of nearly every "20% of global oil" figure in circulation. **Henry Farrell and Abraham Newman** generalised the logic past geography in _weaponized interdependence_ : networks have chokepoints too, and SWIFT is a strait.

Mechanism
    

  1. **Concentration** — no substitute route at comparable cost.
  2. **Cost asymmetry** — closing is cheap, opening is expensive. A drone or a mine costs thousands; escort, minesweeping and underwriting cost billions. This is why small actors hold straits against large navies.
  3. **Third-party dependence** — the pain lands on states not party to the quarrel, which is what turns a local militia into a global actor.
  4. **Transmission** — the underrated condition. Straits are almost never closed; they are _priced_. War-risk insurance does the closing, and premiums are where the power becomes visible.

Where it breaks
    

Denial is not compulsion. Closing Hormuz hurts China and India more than the United States, so the holder's leverage often points at the wrong audience.

Adaptation erodes it on a timescale of months: bypass pipelines, ship-to-ship transfer, dark fleets, re-routing, demand destruction.

And it is usually self-harming, because the holder generally needs the same water. Iran exports through Hormuz too.

## The strongest objection
    

That chokepoints are a cartographer's illusion. The record of actual closure is close to empty. Hormuz has never been closed — not even during the 1984–88 tanker war, when hundreds of ships were hit. Suez closed in 1956 and again from 1967 to 1975, and world trade re-routed around the Cape at a cost measured in freight rates rather than strategic defeat; it produced the supertanker rather than a catastrophe. The Ever Given blocked Suez for six days in 2021 and the result was a spike and a shrug.

On this reading, what we call chokepoint power is the _anticipated_ version of it — an insurance market pricing a tail risk that almost never arrives. It is a theory of premiums, not of power, and states that build fleets around it are buying the wrong navy.

**The reply.** The objection describes the mechanism accurately and then mislabels it a refutation. The holder does not need to close the strait; it needs closure to be credible enough to be priced, and then to collect. The Houthis are not closing Bab al-Mandeb — they are selling differential access, exempting everyone except the party they are fighting. Never closing is the optimal strategy, not evidence against the theory. And the tanker war did not end because denial failed; it ended when the United States reflagged Kuwaiti tankers and paid the escort bill. Someone absorbed the cost. That is exactly what Washington declined to do for Riyadh in September 2026, which is why the 1987 precedent now cuts the other way.

Cases
    

  * **Operation Earnest Will, 1987** — Kuwaiti tankers reflagged under US colours and escorted through the tanker war. The precedent is not that the strait stayed open; it is about who pays for it to stay open.
  * **Suez, 1956 and 1967–75** — closure, Cape re-routing, and the birth of the VLCC. Infrastructure adapts to geography on a decade timescale.
  * **The Malacca dilemma** — named by Hu Jintao in 2003. Two decades and many billions later the answer is Gwadar, Kyaukphyu, Arctic routing, strategic reserves and a blue-water navy. That is what taking a chokepoint seriously looks like as a national programme, and a useful benchmark for what India has and has not built.
  * **The Turkish Straits** — the Montreux Convention of 1936 turned chokepoint power into treaty law. Turkey's leverage in 2022 came from a document, not a gun.

The open question
    

Watch war-risk premiums while the Houthis still hold Perim Island. If rates fall with the militia in place, chokepoint power is thinner than it looks and the sceptics are right. If they stay elevated, the world has quietly accepted a toll booth — and the question becomes who collects, and who pays.
