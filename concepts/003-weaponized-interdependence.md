# 003 · Weaponized interdependence
*Filed 17 September 2026 — the Graham Act reached the president's desk with authority to tariff buyers of Russian energy at up to 100%.*

#### Definition

The networks through which states trade, pay, insure and communicate are not flat. They have hubs. A state with legal jurisdiction over a hub can convert everyone else's connection into a vulnerability. Interdependence was supposed to make coercion harder by making everyone mutually hostage; this is the finding that it did the opposite for whoever sits on the central node.

#### Lineage

**Henry Farrell and Abraham Newman**, "Weaponized Interdependence: How Global Economic Networks Shape State Coercion," _International Security_ (2019); book-length as _Underground Empire_ (2023).

The ancestor is **Albert Hirschman**, _National Power and the Structure of Foreign Trade_ (1945), written about Nazi Germany's trade with the Balkans: trade asymmetry is itself a form of power. **Robert Keohane and Joseph Nye**, _Power and Interdependence_ (1977), supplied the optimistic reading Farrell and Newman invert — they had asymmetric interdependence as leverage, but expected it diffuse and reciprocal.

What Farrell and Newman added is **topology**: real networks are scale-free rather than evenly connected, so asymmetry concentrates in a few places, and those places sit inside somebody's legal jurisdiction. **Daniel Drezner**'s sanctions literature supplies the empirical discipline, most of it deflationary.

#### Mechanism

Two effects, and the distinction does the work.

1. **The panopticon effect** — the hub lets you _see_ the flows. SWIFT messaging told Washington who was paying whom long before it told anyone to stop.
2. **The chokepoint effect** — the hub lets you _cut someone off_: dollar clearing, SWIFT, top-level DNS, TSMC and ASML, the London and New York hull-underwriting markets.

Three enabling conditions: the network must be **centralised**; the hub must sit in **one state's jurisdiction**, because this power is legal before it is economic, exercised through subpoenas rather than fleets; and the target must have **no substitute at tolerable cost**, which is the same concentration condition as concept 002. Farrell and Newman generalised chokepoint geopolitics off the map. A strait is a network hub that happens to be made of water.

#### Where it breaks

Every use depreciates the asset, because coercion teaches the target to build an alternative: CIPS, rupee and yuan settlement, non-dollar oil pricing, domestic fabs.

It needs allies, since the chokepoint is often jointly held — SWIFT is Belgian, ASML Dutch — so unilateral use burns coalition capital.

It is blunt, landing on populations and firms with no influence over the policy at issue. And it invites a counter-chokepoint; export licensing on rare earths is the demonstration.

#### The strongest objection

That weaponized interdependence describes a transitional decade rather than a structure of power. Its mechanism is jurisdiction over hubs; hubs are artifacts of efficiency; and once political risk is priced into the hub, efficiency stops selecting for it. Every deployment therefore accelerates the weapon's own obsolescence. The 2012 Iranian cutoff produced barter and gold. The 2022 Russian cutoff produced roughly 90% national-currency settlement inside BRICS trade within four years. Semiconductor controls produced a Chinese domestic fabrication programme with a national budget behind it. A weapon that degrades with every firing is not leverage; it is a stock being liquidated.

And note the hidden cost to the wielder: every flow that exits the system is a flow the Treasury can no longer see, which means _the chokepoint effect destroys the panopticon effect_. On this reading Farrell and Newman described a hegemon eating its own instruments.

#### The reply

Right about the mechanism, wrong about the timescale — and the timescale is the whole argument. Substitution is real and slow; coercion is real and fast. CIPS has operated for a decade and still clears a small fraction of dollar volume. Russian firms hold rupee balances they cannot convert, which is what substitution actually looks like: not an alternative system, but an accumulation of unusable money. The correct inference is not that the weapon is useless but that it is a **depreciating asset** — which changes how it should be used, not whether it works.

Hence the significance of discretionary design. A tariff authority that is _held_ rather than fired does not depreciate. It extracts compliance without spending the asset: the panopticon preserved by declining to use the chokepoint. Whoever drafted the Graham Act's discretion understood the objection better than most of the bill's critics.

#### Cases

  * **SWIFT and Iran, 2012** — a European regulation compelled a Belgian messaging cooperative to expel Iranian banks, and Iranian oil revenue became unbankable. The usual lesson is that it worked. The better lesson is that it required an ally to pass a law.
  * **Semiconductors and the Foreign Direct Product Rule** — a supply-chain rather than payment-system chokepoint, enforced through a claim about American tooling inside foreign fabs. The clearest case of jurisdiction, not economics, as the source of the power.
  * **Russian gas to Europe, in reverse** — Moscow held a chokepoint, fired it, and lost the market permanently inside two years. The counter-case, and the best evidence for the depreciation objection.
  * **Rare earths and export licensing** — the map has more than one hub, and the second-largest economy sits on some of them.

#### The open question

Whether the Graham Act is used or held. If the tariff lands on India, the theory's critics get their best datapoint and India gets a reason to build. If it sits unexercised through the midterms and beyond, the instrument keeps its value and the deterrence works silently — and the lesson is that the most effective economic coercion is the kind that never appears in a trade statistic.
