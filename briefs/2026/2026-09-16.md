## The one thing

American officials met Houthi representatives at the US embassy in Muscat on Sunday, days after the militia seized Yemen's entire Red Sea coastline and Perim Island in the Bab al-Mandeb. The Houthis told them they would not attack American, Israeli or commercial vessels — only Saudi ones. In the same week, Mohammed bin Salman phoned Trump asking for military help against the Houthi advance and Washington said no. The group holding the chokepoint got a negotiation. The seventy-year ally got a refusal.

## What moved

### Washington negotiated a carve-out with the militia that now owns Bab al-Mandeb

Reuters, citing five sources, reports the meeting took place on Sunday at the US embassy in Muscat, organised with Omani help. The Houthi delegation included **Mohammad Abdulsalam** , a senior official under US sanctions, and Abdelmalik al-Ajri. The Houthis said they remain committed to the May 2025 ceasefire with the United States and would attack no commercial shipping _except vessels belonging to Saudi Arabia_. A State Department spokesperson declined to confirm the meeting, saying only that freedom of navigation in the Red Sea is a core US interest.

Context the wire supplies and that matters: the Trump administration designated the Houthis a foreign terrorist organisation in March 2025, then bombed them for two months, then signed a ceasefire with them in May 2025. Fighting resumed in July 2026 when the Houthis declared a blockade on Saudi shipping in answer to a Saudi blockade of Yemeni ports. Last week they took the port of Mocha, the whole Red Sea coast, and Perim Island at the mouth of the strait.

_Why it matters:_ A proscribed organisation that controls a strait is now a counterparty, and its sanctioned negotiator sat inside a US diplomatic mission to be one. More important than the venue is the deal's shape. The Houthis have separated **freedom of navigation** from **the protection of Saudi Arabia** , and Washington has in practice accepted the distinction: it will take an exemption for its own hulls and decline to fight for its ally's. That is not an American withdrawal from the Gulf. It is a repricing of what the United States will pay to keep a waterway open on someone else's behalf.

### Four ships

Reuters vessel-tracking data for **16 September** : four transits of the Strait of Hormuz — two in, two out — down from seven the previous day against a ten-day average of eighteen. No very large crude carriers and no LNG tankers at all. The two outbound vessels were a gas carrier with roughly 470,000 barrels of LPG and a Panamax carrying about 510,000 barrels of naphtha on a dark route. Bab al-Mandeb ran 22 transits, down from 24. Reuters notes some ships move with transponders off, so the count understates rather than overstates.

Correction

Yesterday's brief carried the single-digit transit figure as Al Jazeera's and flagged it as not wire-confirmed, while giving the US Energy Secretary's two-thirds-of-pre-war-flows claim a straight reading. Reuters' own tracking data now confirms the single digits. The caution was pointed the wrong way: the thin-sourced number was right and the official number was the one that needed the hedge. Both cannot describe the same waterway.

Saudi Arabia responded by re-plumbing its export system. Aramco is offering Asian refiners crude by ship-to-ship transfer off **Sohar, Oman — outside the strait** — and has roughly doubled daily loadings at Ras Tanura and Juaymah to about 4 million barrels, with four VLCCs carrying a combined 8 million barrels loading at Ras Tanura on Wednesday. Yanbu loadings on the Red Sea are suspended and some September cargoes for European buyers face cancellation, after the East-West pipeline was shut on Friday with drone damage.

_The wires disagree on the repair, so both readings stand:_ Reuters reports the US energy secretary saying the pipeline should be back online within days; AP reports it will be out of service for weeks. Riyadh's own behaviour is the more informative signal — you do not rebuild your loading programme around Oman for a problem measured in days.

And the bill arrived. The Congressional Budget Office puts the cost of the Iran war at **$38 billion through 1 August** , rising about $3 billion a month, with 18 US service members killed. Munitions are the dominant line, and the CBO's judgement is the part to keep: it could take **five years to replenish US stockpiles**. Defense Secretary Hegseth has asked for nearly $90 billion in emergency restocking funds. For scale, Iraq ran about $2 trillion over eight years.

_Why it matters:_ The strait is not closed. It is priced, thinly used, and being routed around — which is what a chokepoint under pressure actually looks like. The five-year magazine is the line with the longest reach: ordnance spent in the Gulf is ordnance unavailable in the Western Pacific until 2031. For an Indian reader that is the most consequential sentence in this brief. The Indo-Pacific balance India hedges between is being set, right now, by expenditure in a different ocean.

### The forced choice acquired a bill number

The House took up the **Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026** , which passed the Senate 86–11 in August and which Trump backs. It sanctions Russian officials and authorises tariffs of **up to 100%** on countries deemed major consumers of Russian energy — China, India, Japan and several European allies — at the president's discretion to impose and to lift.

"If the House passes this bill as written, the result will not be sanctions on Russia or support for Ukraine, it will be higher costs and more inflation for the American people."

Rep. Gregory Meeks (D-NY), to Reuters

_Why it matters:_ Yesterday's concept ended by saying multi-alignment is a bet that you never face a forced choice. The bet is now being priced in legislative text, aimed at a country Washington signed a trade deal with in February. Read the design rather than the headline: the tariff is discretionary, not automatic. That is not a sanctions regime, it is a permanent lever — the value is in holding it, indefinitely, over a country that buys about a third of its crude from Russia. Discretion is the product.

### The United States said out loud that it has weapons in orbit

Air Force Secretary **Troy Meink** said on Monday that the US "has on-orbit space control weapons capable of defending the Joint Force against hostile adversary action." Space Force's **Douglas Schiess** said on Tuesday that enlisted members "operate on-orbit weapons that can defend the Joint Force against space-enabled attacks." Neither would describe the weapons. US Space Command's Richard Palmer called the acknowledgement "fundamental to ensuring that we prevent deterrence from failing." China's foreign ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun said Beijing "opposes any arms race in outer space or any attempt to weaponize it"; Russia's foreign ministry said the admission threatened long-term stability in space activity.

_Why it matters:_ The capability is not the news — everyone assumed it. The _declaration_ is the news, and it was the instrument. Deterrence only works on an adversary who knows, which is why Palmer framed disclosure as deterrence maintenance. What ends here is a sixty-year convention of strategic ambiguity in which the major powers armed orbit and declined to say so. Ambiguity is a cheap form of restraint: it costs nothing and it lets everyone avoid responding. Once one party names its capability, the others are under domestic pressure to name or match theirs. Watch for a Chinese or Russian counter- declaration; that would be the confirmation that a norm rather than a secret was what just broke.

### Trump tried to switch off the Ukraine energy war; neither side stopped

Trump called on Ukraine to halt strikes on Russian diesel facilities, citing the global shortage. The Kremlin welcomed the idea of an energy truce and asked for sanctions relief in return. Zelenskiy said Ukraine would support an energy ceasefire if Washington could vouch for Moscow's intent. Reuters then reported that both sides pressed on with energy strikes regardless, and that **half of Russia's six largest diesel-producing refineries** cut back sharply or halted output in September because of drone damage — a Reuters calculation from fuel-market participants. Diesel is near a record high. Russian drones killed five people on a bus and a train in Ukraine on Wednesday.

_Why it matters:_ This is the first time the most effective campaign in the war has been the target of American diplomacy, and the stated reason is the price of diesel rather than the position of Ukraine. The awkwardness is structural: Ukraine's deep-strike campaign is working — that is precisely why Washington wants it stopped — which makes it the single hardest thing to ask Kyiv to give up, and the most valuable thing Moscow could be sold. The thread's status changes today: territory is still static, but the war's real theory of victory has entered negotiation.

### Indian and Pakistani warships collided in the north Arabian Sea

A Pakistani naval vessel and an Indian frontline warship on routine surveillance collided in international waters on Tuesday. Indian sources report no major damage. India's foreign ministry summoned Pakistan's senior diplomat in Delhi, calling the conduct "unacceptable and unprofessional" and "in direct contravention" of the 1991 bilateral agreement requiring naval vessels to keep at least three nautical miles apart. Pakistan's government and military had not responded when Reuters filed. It is the first overt military friction between the two since last year's four-day conflict.

_Why it matters:_ Not because a hull was scraped. The 1991 agreement is a confidence-building measure, and a confidence-building measure has no enforcement, no penalty and no value except being observed. Its entire function is to keep an accident from becoming a crisis between nuclear-armed neighbours. Something designed to make this category of event boring just failed at that job, a year after a shooting war. Whether this was a helmsman or a signal is the question, and both India and Pakistan will be asking it.

## Concept of the day — chokepoint geopolitics

### The plain version

Chokepoint geopolitics is the study of how control over a narrow passage that disproportionate traffic must use converts into political power — and, just as importantly, why that power is far easier to use for denial than for gain. The holder of a strait can impose costs on the world. It cannot generally make the world do anything.

### Where it comes from

The founding text is **Alfred Thayer Mahan** , _The Influence of Sea Power Upon History_ (1890): naval supremacy rests not on territory but on holding the narrow seas through which commerce must pass. The British imperial system was the applied version — Gibraltar, Suez, Aden, Malacca, Hormuz, the Cape — a chain of locks rather than a map of colonies. **Halford Mackinder** (1904) supplied the land-power counterargument, that railways would make the Eurasian interior immune to sea control; **Nicholas Spykman** split the difference with the rimland. The modern empirical frame is the US Energy Information Administration's _World Oil Transit Chokepoints_ series, which is where nearly every "20% of global oil" figure in circulation originates. **Henry Farrell and Abraham Newman** generalised the logic beyond geography in _weaponized interdependence_ : networks have chokepoints too, and SWIFT is a strait.

### The mechanism

Four conditions have to hold together. **Concentration** — no substitute route at comparable cost. **Cost asymmetry** — closing is cheap and opening is expensive; a drone or a mine costs thousands, while escort, minesweeping and underwriting cost billions, which is why small actors can hold straits against large navies. **Third-party dependence** — the pain lands on states not party to the quarrel, which is what turns a local militia into a global actor. And **a transmission mechanism** , which is the underrated one: straits are almost never closed, they are _priced_. War-risk insurance does the closing. Premiums, not blockades, are where chokepoint power shows up.

### Where it strains

Denial is not compulsion — shutting Hormuz hurts China and India more than the United States, so the holder's leverage frequently points at the wrong audience. Adaptation erodes it: bypass pipelines, ship-to-ship transfers, dark fleets, re-routing and demand destruction all work, on a timescale of months. And it is usually self-harming, because the holder generally needs the same water. Iran exports through Hormuz too.

### The strongest objection, in its best form

That chokepoints are a cartographer's illusion. The record of actual closure is close to empty: Hormuz has never been closed, not even during the 1984–88 tanker war, when hundreds of ships were hit. Suez closed in 1956 and again from 1967 to 1975, and world trade re-routed around the Cape at a cost measured in freight rates rather than strategic defeat — it produced the supertanker rather than a catastrophe. The Ever Given blocked Suez for six days in 2021 and the effect was a spike and a shrug. On this reading, what we call chokepoint power is really the anticipated version of it, an insurance market pricing a tail risk that almost never arrives. Chokepoint geopolitics would then be a theory of premiums, not of power — and states that build fleets around it are buying the wrong navy.

### The reply

The objection describes the mechanism accurately and then mislabels it a refutation. The holder does not need to close the strait. It needs closure to be credible enough to be priced, and then to collect. Today is the cleanest possible illustration: the Houthis are not closing Bab al-Mandeb, they are **selling differential access** — everyone passes except the party they are fighting. Never closing is the optimal strategy, not evidence against the theory. As for the tanker war, it did not end because denial failed; it ended when the United States reflagged Kuwaiti tankers and paid the escort bill. Someone absorbed the cost. That is precisely what Washington declined to do for Riyadh this week, which is why the 1984–88 precedent cuts the other way now.

### Cases beyond today

**The tanker war and Operation Earnest Will, 1987.** The US reflagged Kuwaiti tankers under American colours and escorted them. The precedent is not that the strait stayed open; it is about who pays for it to stay open.

**Suez, 1956 and 1967–75.** Closure, re-routing around the Cape, and the economic answer — the very large crude carrier, a ship built because a canal became unreliable. Infrastructure adapts to geography on a decade timescale.

**The Malacca dilemma.** Hu Jintao named it in 2003: most of China's oil passes a strait the US Navy could close. Two decades and many billions later the answer is Gwadar, Kyaukphyu, Arctic routing, strategic reserves and a blue-water navy. That is what taking a chokepoint seriously looks like as a national programme — and it is a useful benchmark for what India has and has not built.

**The Turkish Straits.** The Montreux Convention of 1936 turned chokepoint power into treaty law. Turkey's real leverage in 2022 came from a document, not a gun — a reminder that legal control of a passage is a form of it.

### What would settle it

Watch war-risk premiums while the Houthis still hold Perim Island. If rates fall with the militia in place, chokepoint power is thinner than it looks and the sceptics are right. If they stay elevated, the world has quietly accepted a toll booth, and the question becomes who collects and who pays.

## Sit with this

The United States spent seventy years building a Gulf security order whose core promise was that America keeps the sea lanes open for its friends. This week it declined to fight for its friend's shipping and obtained an exemption for its own. Nothing was announced. A meeting simply happened in Muscat, and the State Department declined to confirm it.

Consider what an exemption is. It is an acknowledgement that the strait has an owner, and that the owner takes appointments.

Then ask the India question. India has no navy in the Red Sea, no carve-out of its own, and supplies a very large share of the world's merchant seafarers. In a world of _guaranteed_ passage, a middle power can free-ride on the guarantee and spend its diplomacy elsewhere. In a world of _negotiated_ passage, every state buys access individually, and the price is paid in alignment. Multi-alignment is a strategy built for a world of guarantees under strain. It has much less to say about a world of tolls.

And the hardest part: the answer depends on whether Washington declined because it could not or because it would not. From Riyadh, and from Delhi, those look exactly the same.
