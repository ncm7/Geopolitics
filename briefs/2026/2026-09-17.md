## The one thing

Saudi Arabia has run low on interceptor missiles and has gone asking France, Britain, Pakistan and Egypt for air defence — because its supplier of fifty years is short too. The United States burned through its own stocks in six months of war with Iran. None of the four has committed. Yesterday this brief asked whether Washington declined to defend Saudi shipping because it would not or because it could not. Part of the answer is now on the record, and it is the second one.

## What moved

### Riyadh went shopping for a patron and nobody has said yes

AP, citing regional officials: Saudi Arabia has requested air-defence assistance from **France, Britain, Pakistan and Egypt**. Its interceptor stockpile has been drawn down by the Houthi campaign, and one official said the United States — the kingdom's principal weapons supplier — is itself short after six months of fighting Iran. Britain's prime minister's office confirmed a "close defence partnership" with the kingdom but would not say whether a request had been made or whether British forces are deployed there. Turkey and Pakistan both said they had received no formal request, while issuing statements of solidarity; a Turkish official said Ankara is talking to Iran and the UAE about de-escalation. Vice President **JD Vance** said Washington is in "constant contact" with Saudi officials and has held "direct conversations with the Houthis." Riyadh has called an intercepted drone near Mecca — roughly 680 miles from the Yemeni border — a "red line." The Houthis deny that attack.

> **Resolved, partly.** Yesterday's closer asked whether Washington declined to defend Saudi shipping because it would not or because it could not, and said that from Riyadh the two look identical. They are not identical, and the larger share now looks like *could not*. Escorting is still a decision rather than an inventory, so "would not" has not gone away — but this brief weighted it too heavily.

Pakistan's position deserves a second look. On **7 August 2026** Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and Turkey signed the **Mecca Joint Defence Agreement**, whose text holds that an armed attack on any one of the three is an attack on all — Article 5 language, built on the September 2025 Saudi–Pakistani Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement, with a permanent secretariat seated in Saudi Arabia. Pakistan has some 8,000 troops and air force assets at King Abdulaziz Air Base and has joined the Saudi-led maritime alliance. It has not fired at the Houthis. After the 8 September missile and drone barrage on Saudi infrastructure, a Pakistani foreign ministry spokesman, Sajjad Haider Khan, said of operations against the group: *"There is no discussion of this kind."* Pakistan shares a 900-kilometre border with Iran, which is the reason. *(Internal detail on the pact's gaps from Middle East Eye, Lean Left per Ground News — the only outlet carrying it; the treaty text, the deployments and the spokesman's line are separately documented.)*

> **CORRECTION.** Yesterday's brief read the CBO's five-year munitions-replenishment estimate as a constraint with a long fuse — "ordnance spent in the Gulf is ordnance unavailable in the Western Pacific until 2031." The direction was right and the timing was wrong. The shortage is binding *now*, on a current ally in a current war, not in a future contingency. A magazine does not empty on the date the replenishment schedule ends. It empties when the next customer asks.

**Why it matters.** An alliance is being revealed as a supply contract, and the supplier is out of stock. That is a different failure from abandonment and in one respect a worse one, because resolve cannot fix it. Three things follow. Extended deterrence rests on **inventory, not intent**, and inventory is now the binding constraint across two theatres at once. A collective-defence treaty six weeks old has produced troops, ships and communiqués but no shots — which is what most collective-defence treaties produce, and worth remembering the next time one is signed. And look at whom Riyadh asked: Europe's two serious expeditionary powers plus two large Muslim armies. That is not a hedge against the United States. It is an attempt to reconstitute a guarantee out of spare parts.

### The forced choice stopped being a bill

The House passed the **Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026** on 16 September by **262–159**, with 58 Democrats joining most Republicans, over the objections of Democratic leadership. The Senate passed it 86–11 in August. It now goes to the president's desk. The act authorises tariffs of **up to 100%** on major importers of Russian oil and gas — China and India named — sanctions Russian officials, companies and financial institutions, and extends Iran sanctions through **2031**.

> "This bill has a loophole that would allow him to define basically any country as a facilitator of evading Russian sanctions."
> — Rep. Don Beyer (D-VA)

India's Ministry of External Affairs answered the same day. "India remains firmly committed to ensuring energy security for its 1.4 billion people. It will continue to do so through **diversified sourcing**." The statement added that the bill's "potential implications for not just the bilateral relationship but also the international energy market have been very clearly articulated," and that the government will work with Indian trade and industry bodies on the consequences.

**Why it matters.** Yesterday this was a bill in a chamber. Today it is a loaded instrument on the desk of a president running a midterm, and the discretion this brief flagged on Wednesday is now law rather than draft. Read the Indian statement closely, because it concedes nothing and threatens nothing. *Diversified sourcing* is the operative phrase: not a defence of the Russian barrel, but a refusal to characterise the purchase as a commitment. That is multi-alignment's reply to a forced choice — deny that a choice is being offered and keep buying. Whether the reply holds depends on the one variable Delhi does not control, which is whether the tariff is fired or held. Beyer's objection is the sharper one and it is not about Russia at all: a definitional loophole means the instrument can be pointed at anyone, which is the whole design.

### A war in the Gulf set the American policy rate

The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark by a quarter point to **3.75–4.00%** on 16 September, unanimously — the first increase in three years. Chair **Kevin Warsh**: "Inflation remains elevated. Today's policy action will support a timelier return to the Committee's 2% goal." Annual inflation ran **3.4%** in August. Diesel set a record at **$6.396 a gallon**; Brent closed at **$103.48**, down 2.2%, and WTI at $100.65, against a pre-war level near $72. Policymakers project one more quarter-point this year and none in 2027, though Warsh has discouraged forward guidance as a matter of preference.

**Why it matters.** Trace the belt. Hormuz to diesel, diesel to American CPI, CPI to the Fed, the Fed to the global cost of capital, and the cost of capital to Indian equities and the rupee. The Nifty's five-month low earlier this week was the far end of that belt, and it is why this system keeps a macro thread at all: a chokepoint 2,000km from Delhi is now setting Indian borrowing costs through Washington rather than directly. Note also what it does to the politics. An administration with an implicit target of oil under $100 now has a monetary authority acting on the same variable, which raises the price of any Gulf escalation before 3 November and lowers the price of a settlement. Trump said this week that he hopes the war ends soon and that Iran has contacted him wanting a deal. Treat the claim as a claim. Treat the incentive as real.

### A UN mission found grounds to believe the United States committed war crimes in Iran — and that Iran committed crimes against humanity against Iranians

The independent fact-finding mission established by the Human Rights Council in 2022, chaired by the Bangladeshi jurist **Sara Hossain**, found "reasonable grounds" that two American strikes on **28 February 2026** amounted to "launching indiscriminate attacks resulting in the loss of life or injury to civilians or damage to civilian objects." At Minab, the Shajareh Tayyebeh school was struck; Iranian state media reported **168 deaths, mostly children**, and the mission concluded the school building "was the intended point of impact." At Lamerd, a sports complex and residential area were hit with Precision Strike Missiles carrying tungsten pellets, with 22 civilians reported killed. At least 178 civilian deaths across the two. US Central Command has denied responsibility for Lamerd; the State Department did not immediately comment.

The same report found killings by Iranian security forces on a "staggering scale," alongside torture, arbitrary detention and extrajudicial killings, and identified more than 70 people at imminent risk of execution. Iran did not answer nearly three dozen inquiries from the mission. *The casualty figures originate with Iranian state media and are carried by the mission rather than independently verified; treat them as contested.*

**Why it matters.** The mission has no enforcement power and will get none. Its product is a record, and records operate on a long timescale — they are the raw material of later proceedings, of third states' justifications, and of the domestic arguments belligerents have with themselves a decade on. The structural feature here is the symmetry: one body, one report, findings against the intervening power and against the regime it fought. That symmetry is what will give the document durability, and it is also precisely why neither government will engage with it. Each can dismiss the half aimed at itself by pointing at the half aimed at the other.

### Israel tendered 2,167 more homes in E1

Rights groups report a new tender adding **2,167 housing units** to the E1 settlement plan east of Jerusalem. Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich has said the project would "bury" a Palestinian state; Mahmoud Abbas condemned it as a violation of international law. Separately, the State Department denied visas to Palestinian officials for a second consecutive year, keeping Abbas from the General Assembly.

**Why it matters.** E1 is the one piece of construction in this conflict that is decisive geographically rather than politically. It severs the northern West Bank from the southern and cuts both from East Jerusalem. Almost everything else about the two-state question is reversible by a signature; this part is reversible only by demolition. Note the sourcing, which is typical of the beat: the count comes from rights groups rather than from the government, which is how most settlement numbers reach the wires at all.

## Concept of the day — weaponized interdependence

**The plain version.** The networks through which states trade, pay, insure and communicate are not flat. They have hubs. A state with legal jurisdiction over a hub can convert everyone else's connection into a vulnerability. Interdependence was supposed to make coercion harder by making everyone mutually hostage. Weaponized interdependence is the finding that it did the opposite for whoever sits on the central node.

**Where it comes from.** The naming text is **Henry Farrell and Abraham Newman**, "Weaponized Interdependence: How Global Economic Networks Shape State Coercion," *International Security* (2019); the book-length version is *Underground Empire* (2023). The ancestor is **Albert Hirschman**, *National Power and the Structure of Foreign Trade* (1945), written about Nazi Germany's trade with the Balkans, which established that trade asymmetry is itself a form of power. **Robert Keohane and Joseph Nye**, *Power and Interdependence* (1977), supplied the optimistic reading that Farrell and Newman invert: they had asymmetric interdependence as leverage but expected it to be diffuse and reciprocal. What Farrell and Newman added is *topology* — real networks are scale-free rather than evenly connected, so the asymmetry concentrates in a few places, and those places sit inside somebody's legal jurisdiction. **Daniel Drezner**'s sanctions literature supplies the empirical discipline, most of it deflationary.

**The mechanism.** Two effects, and the distinction between them does most of the work. The **panopticon effect**: sitting on the hub lets you see the flows. SWIFT messaging told Washington who was paying whom long before it told anyone to stop. The **chokepoint effect**: sitting on the hub lets you cut someone off — dollar clearing, SWIFT, the top-level domain system, TSMC and ASML in semiconductors, the London and New York markets that underwrite hulls.

Three conditions enable it. The network must be **centralised** rather than distributed. The hub must sit in **one state's jurisdiction**, because this power is legal before it is economic — it is exercised through subpoenas and regulations, not fleets. And the target must have **no substitute at tolerable cost**, which is the same concentration condition as yesterday's straits. That is the direct link between the two concepts: Farrell and Newman generalised chokepoint geopolitics off the map. A strait is a network hub that happens to be made of water.

**Where it breaks.** Every use depreciates the asset, because coercion teaches the target to build an alternative: CIPS, rupee and yuan settlement arrangements, non-dollar oil pricing, domestic fabs. It also requires allies, since the chokepoint is usually jointly held — SWIFT is Belgian, ASML is Dutch — so unilateral use burns coalition capital. It is blunt, landing on populations and firms with no influence over the policy at issue. And it invites the target to develop its own chokepoint, which is what export licensing on rare earths demonstrated.

**The strongest objection, in its best form.** That weaponized interdependence describes a transitional decade rather than a structure of power. Its mechanism is jurisdiction over hubs; hubs are artifacts of efficiency; and once political risk is priced into the hub, efficiency stops selecting for it. On this reading every deployment accelerates the weapon's own obsolescence. The 2012 Iranian cutoff produced barter and gold. The 2022 Russian cutoff produced roughly 90% national-currency settlement inside BRICS trade within four years. Semiconductor controls produced a Chinese domestic fabrication programme with a national budget behind it. A weapon that degrades with every firing is not leverage; it is a stock being liquidated. And note who pays the hidden cost: the Treasury loses visibility with every flow that exits the system, which means the chokepoint effect destroys the panopticon effect. Farrell and Newman have described a hegemon eating its own instruments.

**The reply.** The objection is right about the mechanism and wrong about the timescale, and the timescale is the whole argument. Substitution is real and slow; coercion is real and fast. CIPS has operated for a decade and still clears a small fraction of dollar volume. Russian firms hold rupee balances they cannot convert — which is the point this brief made on Wednesday, and it is what substitution looks like in practice: not an alternative system, but an accumulation of unusable money. The correct inference is not that the weapon is useless but that it is a **depreciating asset**, which changes how it should be used rather than whether it works.

That is exactly why the discretionary design of the act now on the president's desk matters more than its 100% ceiling. A tariff authority that is held rather than fired does not depreciate. It extracts compliance without spending the asset — the panopticon preserved by declining to use the chokepoint. Whoever drafted that provision understood the objection better than most of the bill's critics.

**Cases beyond today.**

- **SWIFT and Iran, 2012.** The clean demonstration: a European regulation compelled a Belgian messaging cooperative to expel Iranian banks, and Iranian oil revenue became unbankable. The lesson usually drawn is that it worked. The lesson worth drawing is that it required an ally to pass a law.
- **Semiconductors and the Foreign Direct Product Rule.** A chokepoint in a supply chain rather than a payment system, enforced through a claim about American tooling inside foreign fabs. The clearest case of jurisdiction rather than economics being the source of the power.
- **Russian gas to Europe, in reverse.** Moscow held a chokepoint, fired it, and lost the market permanently inside two years. The counter-case, and the best single piece of evidence for the depreciation objection.
- **Rare earths and export licensing.** The reminder that the map has more than one hub, and that the second-largest economy sits on some of them.

**What would settle it.** Whether the Graham Act is used or held. If the tariff is imposed on India, the theory's critics get their best datapoint and India gets a reason to build. If it sits unexercised through the midterms and beyond, the instrument keeps its value and the deterrence works silently — and we will have learned that the most effective economic coercion is the kind that never shows up in a trade statistic.

## Sit with this

Riyadh asked four capitals for air defence this week. Two are European powers with expeditionary traditions and thin magazines of their own. Two are large Muslim armies bound to Saudi Arabia by money and, in Pakistan's case, by a treaty six weeks old written in NATO's language. None has said yes.

The reflex is to read this as American decline, and it partly is. But look at what is actually scarce. Not will, not ships, not treaties — interceptors. The thing running out is a manufactured good with a multi-year lead time, and every stock is being drawn down by the same war.

Then hold that next to the second item in this brief. On the same day the Gulf's premier client discovered its patron could not resupply it, Congress handed that patron an economic weapon it can aim at a country of 1.4 billion people. One instrument is depleted and one is loaded. A state that has spent its material power leans harder on its financial power, because that is the lever still in the room.

And then the Indian question, which is not about either story on its own. India buys the barrels the loaded weapon targets, and ships them through water the depleted one no longer guarantees. There is a version of the next decade in which those are the same fact, and the country that has most successfully refused to choose discovers the choice has been restructured around it — no longer "whose side are you on," but "on whose networks do you live, and under whose guns do your cargoes move." Nobody has to ask India anything for that to bind.
