# Day 001 — Tuesday, 15 September 2026

*Revised 15 Sept, evening: re-sourced to Reuters wire copy under the charter's sourcing
rule. Three claims in the original draft were wrong or thinly sourced and are corrected
below, marked where they changed.*

## The one thing

Xi Jinping stood in New Delhi this weekend for the first time in seven years, next to Putin
and Iran's Pezeshkian, at a BRICS summit Modi hosted while holding a trade deal with
Washington signed in February. India ran the room. But read the agenda rather than the
photographs and the summit's substance was largely Chinese — and the bloc India was
convening contains both Iran and the UAE, a Washington ally, which is the real reason a
joint statement was hard. Multi-alignment bought India the venue. What it bought India
inside the venue is the open question.

## What moved

**BRICS adopted the New Delhi Declaration, unanimously.**
The bloc expressed "deep concern" over the Middle East war and called for "maximum
restraint," advocating a multilateral approach and peaceful resolution "through dialogue,
consultation, and diplomacy." It also objected to the rise of "unilateral tariff and
non-tariff measures" that distort trade — aimed at US sanctions on Tehran and Moscow.
The communiqué runs to nearly 18,000 words.

*The cohesion problem, correctly stated:* BRICS contains Iran and the United Arab Emirates
at the same time. The UAE is a Washington ally. Getting those two into one text on a
Middle East war is the actual diplomatic achievement, and Reuters reports Iran and the UAE
both backed the statement. My first draft framed this as India–China rivalry being the
binding constraint. That was the wrong axis.

*Correction:* the original draft said the declaration demanded full UN membership for
Palestine and an end to Israeli violence in Gaza. Reuters' account of the declaration does
not carry that language. It appears in Al Jazeera's account (Lean Left per Ground News).
It may well be in an 18,000-word document; it is not confirmed by wire copy, so it is
attributed rather than asserted.

*Concept:* **multi-alignment** — below.

**The counter-read: the summit may have been India's, but the agenda was China's.**
Xi used it to propose a BRICS AI Open Source Zone that China would lead, a BRICS Special
Economic Zone partnership, and more integrated supply chains across the bloc.

Tanvi Madan of Brookings, to Reuters: the summit let India "demonstrate its diversified
portfolio of partnerships" and be "a voice of the Global South and venue for adversaries
to communicate" — but with tradeoffs, "including bolstering a platform that enables China's
global influence," and hosting Putin and Pezeshkian risks upsetting India's other partners.
Allen Carlson of Cornell, more bluntly: "If BRICS is a meeting of equals, China is the most
equal" — while cautioning that "BRICS proposals tend to be more show than substance."

*Why it matters:* This is the strongest available objection to today's concept, and it
comes from people who study India sympathetically. Hold it against the multi-alignment
case below and see which survives.

**The Kremlin said it does not seek de-dollarisation — and revealed why.**
Dmitry Peskov, days before the summit: Russia "does not seek de-dollarisation" and is open
to every acceptable payment method, adding that some countries use their national currency
as a political tool. In the same remarks: **90% of transactions between Russia and BRICS
nations are already conducted in national currencies.**

*Why it matters:* Both halves are true and the combination is the interesting part. Russia
is not campaigning to displace the dollar; sanctions already displaced it bilaterally, and
Moscow would rather frame that as Washington's doing than its own project. Note the
friction Peskov admitted: Russian firms are accumulating rupees they can't easily spend.
That is what monetary autonomy actually costs, and it is why de-dollarisation stays mostly
rhetorical.

*Correction:* the original draft said BRICS is pushing de-dollarisation, citing Iran's
Pezeshkian on national currencies. Iran's position is not the bloc's. Russia's is the one
that matters, and Russia's is the opposite.

*Concept:* **weaponized interdependence** — queued.

**Oil above $100, and Hormuz talks have stalled.**
Brent at $107.82. The US says the strait is under control and Energy Secretary Chris
Wright claims flows are back to two-thirds of pre-war levels; independent vessel tracking
shows single-digit daily transits against a pre-war norm of 100+ vessels and about 20
million barrels a day (Al Jazeera, Lean Left per Ground News — the transit figures are that
outlet's and are not wire-confirmed). War-risk insurance has gone from 0.25% of hull value
to 3–10%: $3–10 million per transit on a $100 million ship. A drone strike damaged the
Saudi East-West pipeline, the Hormuz bypass, exposing another 4% of global supply.
US Strategic Petroleum Reserve releases have ended.

Reuters this week: Houthis striking Saudi targets anew as Hormuz talks stall; and, against
the alarm, the marine fuel supply squeeze easing as the market adapts to prolonged
disruption.

*Why it matters:* Two things are true — the strait is badly degraded, and markets adapt
faster than headlines suggest. Insurance rates are the honest signal, because underwriters
price risk for money rather than for position.

**The oil shock reached India.** August inflation rose and the oil surge has, per Reuters,
clinched the case for India's first rate hike in nearly four years. The rupee fell with
stocks and bonds.

*Why it matters:* This is the thread that ties the whole brief together. India hosted a
summit about global governance while a chokepoint 2,000 kilometres away set its interest
rate. Modi raised exactly this with Pezeshkian on 11 September, stressing the need to
protect navigation and seafarers. Multi-alignment is not only a diplomatic posture; it is
how a state with no navy in the Gulf tries to get its oil through one.

**The Houthis hold Yemen's Red Sea coastline and Bab al-Mandeb.**
Two maritime chokepoints under the influence of actors hostile to the US-led order, at once.
Reuters frames the consequence precisely: the Houthi advance leaves Gulf states with an
uncomfortable choice.

*Why it matters:* The structural item. Naval escort can reopen a strait; it cannot make
insurers price it as safe.

**Ukraine ground on, and the numbers stopped moving.**
Russia holds roughly 19–20% of Ukraine, about 45,000 square miles. In the four weeks to
8 September it gained 14–75 square miles depending on the count. Over a year: under 900.
Casualty estimates as of early 2026: about 1 million Russian killed and wounded,
250,000–300,000 Ukrainian. Over 80% of Ukraine's power generation destroyed or damaged as
of August. Russian refining capacity badly degraded by Ukrainian strikes. Polling: 59% in
Russia support negotiations, 69% in Ukraine favour a negotiated end.
(Russia Matters War Report Card, 9 Sept, compiling contested figures — treat every casualty
range as contested.)

*Why it matters:* Both publics want out and neither state can accept the terms the other
would offer.

## A note on the coverage itself

Ground News shows BRICS coverage running roughly **58% right-leaning sources**, with the
top outlets by volume being Times of India (Lean Right), Latestly (Right), Tribune India
(Lean Right), Hindustan Times (Center) and India Today (Lean Right).

That skew is mostly an artefact of who covers BRICS at all — Indian outlets dominate a
summit held in Delhi, and the Indian media market leans right of its Western counterparts.
But it means most available English-language copy on this summit was produced by outlets
with a stake in it being a success for India. The Reuters "Advantage China" read above is
the kind of thing that skew tends to bury. Worth remembering the next time a BRICS summit
reads as an unambiguous Indian triumph.

## Concept of the day — multi-alignment

**The plain version.** A state deliberately maintaining working partnerships with rival
powers at the same time, refusing to convert any of them into an exclusive commitment, and
treating that refusal as the source of its leverage rather than a problem to be resolved.

**Where it comes from.** It is the successor to non-alignment, and the distinction is the
whole point. Nehru's Non-Aligned Movement, formalised at Bandung in 1955 and Belgrade in
1961, was a posture of *abstention* — staying out of the Cold War blocs, with a strong
moral and anti-colonial charge. Multi-alignment is a posture of *participation*: be in the
Quad and in BRICS, buy Russian oil and American aircraft, host Xi and sign with Washington.
Non-alignment said "neither." Multi-alignment says "both, and you can't stop me."
C. Raja Mohan and S. Jaishankar have both articulated versions; Jaishankar's *The India Way*
(2020) is the standard reference.

**What makes it work.** Three conditions. Enough market or strategic weight that each
partner wants you more than it wants to punish you. Rivals who are genuine rivals, so each
fears driving you to the other. And domestic politics that can absorb the accusation of
inconsistency. India has all three. Turkey has tried the play with less weight — S-400s
from Russia while in NATO, and it paid, losing the F-35. Most states have none of the three
and must pick.

**Where it strains.** It is a bet that you will never face a forced choice: cheap in a
competitive world, expensive in a bipolar one. If US–China competition hardens past a
point — a Taiwan contingency is the obvious trigger — the space between the poles
compresses and the hedge stops paying.

**The strongest objection, in its best form.** Not that multi-alignment is incoherent, but
that it is *load-bearing for someone else*. Madan's version: convening adversaries builds a
platform, and the platform's main beneficiary may not be the convenor. Carlson's version:
in a bloc of unequals, the largest economy writes the agenda regardless of who hosts.
On this reading India did not demonstrate leverage this weekend; it supplied a venue,
legitimacy and a photograph to a Chinese project, and took the reputational cost with
Washington for doing it.

**The reply.** That this assumes influence is zero-sum between host and largest member.
India's actual objective may be narrower and more defensible: keep a channel to Beijing
open after a border conflict, keep Russia from becoming a Chinese dependency, and keep
Washington aware that neither relationship is exclusive. Carlson's own caveat — BRICS
proposals are "more show than substance" — cuts both ways. If the AI zone and the SEZ
partnership never materialise, China got a photograph too.

**What would settle it.** Whether any of Xi's proposals acquire budgets and institutions.
Infrastructure is the tell: if it gets built, Carlson is right that it comes from Beijing.
If it doesn't, the summit was theatre and India got the cheap version of what it wanted.

## Sit with this

India hosted a summit of a bloc whose two most powerful members are the states its American
security relationship exists to balance against, and got a unanimous text out of a
membership that includes both Iran and a US ally. That is real diplomatic skill.

And the agenda that text was wrapped around was largely written in Beijing.

So: when a middle power convenes the great powers, who is using whom? The realist answer is
that venues are not power and India rented out its legitimacy. The answer from Indian
strategic thinking is that the ability to convene *is* the power, because it is the thing
neither Washington nor Beijing can do right now.

Notice that you cannot settle this by looking at the summit. You settle it by watching
whether anything proposed there gets built, and by whom.
