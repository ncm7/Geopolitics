#### State of play

A Pakistani naval vessel and an Indian frontline warship on routine surveillance collided in international waters in the north Arabian Sea on Tuesday 15 September. Indian sources report no major damage. India's Ministry of External Affairs summoned Pakistan's senior diplomat, calling the conduct "unacceptable and unprofessional" and "in direct contravention" of the **1991 bilateral agreement** requiring naval vessels of the two countries to maintain at least three nautical miles of separation. Pakistan had not responded publicly when Reuters filed. This is the first overt military friction between the two since last year's four-day conflict and the ceasefire that ended it.

#### Why it is structural

The 1991 agreement is a confidence-building measure. It carries no penalty and no enforcement; its entire value is that both navies observe it, so that a bad night at sea stays a bad night at sea. Mechanisms like this are what stand between an accident and a crisis between nuclear-armed neighbours, and they degrade quietly — a breach that produces no casualties and no headlines is exactly how the erosion begins.

#### The ambiguity that matters

Nothing yet distinguishes a helmsman's error from a deliberate signal, and the two require opposite responses. Watch the Pakistani account when it comes: an apology or a technical explanation reads one way; a counter-accusation of Indian shadowing reads another. Watch also whether either navy changes its surveillance patterns, which is the response that costs something.

#### What would count as real change

  * A second incident, which would end the accident reading.
  * Either side formally suspending or repudiating the 1991 arrangement.
  * A change in deployment patterns in the north Arabian Sea.
  * Any linkage to the Gulf war — both navies are operating in more crowded water than usual because of it.
